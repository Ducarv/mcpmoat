# AI Writing Tropes Reference

This is the full catalog of AI writing patterns to detect and eliminate. Organized by category for quick scanning during edits.

---

## Word Choice

### "Quietly" and Magic Adverbs
Adverbs used to inject false significance: "quietly", "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "arguably".
- "quietly orchestrating workflows" → cut the adverb or rewrite
- "a quiet intelligence behind it" → say what it actually does

### "Delve" and Friends
Overused AI vocabulary: "delve", "certainly", "utilize", "leverage" (as verb), "robust", "streamline", "harness".
- Replace with plain equivalents: use, strong, simplify, take advantage of

### "Tapestry" and "Landscape"
Grandiose nouns where simpler words work: "tapestry", "landscape", "paradigm", "synergy", "ecosystem", "framework".
- "the rich tapestry of human experience" → cut entirely or say what you mean
- "navigating the complex landscape of modern AI" → "working with modern AI"

### The "Serves As" Dodge
Pompous copula replacements: "serves as", "stands as", "marks", "represents" where "is" works fine.
- "The building serves as a reminder" → "The building is a reminder" (or better: just state the reminder)

## Sentence Structure

### Negative Parallelism
"It's not X — it's Y" pattern. The single most recognized AI tell. One per piece max; zero is better.
- "It's not bold. It's backwards." → State your actual point directly.
- Includes: "not because X, but because Y", the em-dash dismissal "X — not Y", cross-sentence reframe "The question isn't X. The question is Y."

### "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."
Dramatic countdown negation. Builds false tension.
- "Not a bug. Not a feature. A fundamental design flaw." → "It's a design flaw."

### "The X? A Y."
Self-posed rhetorical question answered immediately.
- "The result? Devastating." → "The result was devastating." (or better: show why)
- "The worst part? Nobody saw it coming."

### Anaphora Abuse
Same sentence opening repeated 3+ times in sequence.
- "They could expose... They could offer... They could provide..." → Vary the structure, combine points.

### Tricolon Abuse
Overuse of rule-of-three, especially back-to-back tricolons or lists extended to 4–5 items.
- One tricolon is fine. Two in a row is suspicious. Three is a pattern failure.

### "It's Worth Noting"
Filler transitions: "It's worth noting", "It bears mentioning", "Importantly", "Interestingly", "Notably".
- Cut the filler. Start with the actual point.

### Superficial Analyses
Present-participle phrases tacked onto sentences for shallow significance: "highlighting its importance", "reflecting broader trends", "contributing to the development of...", "underscoring its role as...".
- Cut the phrase. If the significance matters, give it its own sentence with actual substance.

### False Ranges
"From X to Y" where X and Y aren't on a real spectrum.
- "From innovation to cultural transformation" → These aren't endpoints of a scale. Just list what you mean.

## Paragraph Structure

### Short Punchy Fragments
Excessive one-sentence paragraphs for manufactured emphasis.
- "He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest." → Combine into a real sentence.
- One-word or two-word paragraphs used for drama: "Platforms do." → Fold into surrounding text.

### Listicle in a Trench Coat
Numbered points disguised as prose: "The first wall is... The second wall is... The third wall is..."
- If it's a list, make it a list. If it's prose, write actual connected paragraphs.

## Tone

### "Here's the Kicker"
False suspense transitions: "Here's the kicker", "Here's the thing", "Here's where it gets interesting", "Here's what most people miss", "Here's the deal".
- Cut the buildup. State the point.

### "Think of It As..."
Patronizing analogies: "Think of it like a highway system for data."
- Drop the analogy if the concept is clear without it. If an analogy helps, integrate it naturally.

### "Imagine a World Where..."
Futurism sell: "Imagine a world where every tool you use has a quiet intelligence behind it..."
- State what's possible concretely rather than asking readers to imagine a utopia.

### False Vulnerability
Performative self-awareness: "And yes, I'm openly in love with the platform model."
- Cut. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable. This is decoration.

### "The Truth Is Simple"
Asserting clarity instead of demonstrating it: "The reality is simpler", "History is unambiguous on this point", "History is clear, the metrics are clear, the examples are clear".
- If it's clear, show it. Don't tell the reader it's clear.

### Grandiose Stakes Inflation
Everything is world-historically significant: "This will fundamentally reshape how we think about everything", "will define the next era of computing".
- Scale claims to what the evidence supports.

### "Let's Break This Down"
Pedagogical hand-holding: "Let's break this down", "Let's unpack this", "Let's explore", "Let's dive in".
- Just start explaining. The reader knows what's happening.

### Vague Attributions
Unnamed authorities: "Experts argue...", "Industry reports suggest...", "Observers have cited..."
- Name the source or cut the claim.

### Invented Concept Labels
Made-up compound terms presented as established: "the supervision paradox", "the acceleration trap", "workload creep".
- If the concept isn't established, describe it in plain language. Don't name-drop your own invention.

## Formatting

### Em-Dash Addiction
20+ em dashes per piece. Humans use 2–3 naturally.
- Replace most with commas, parentheses, or sentence breaks.

### Bold-First Bullets
Every bullet starts with a bolded keyword. Classic AI documentation tell.
- "**Security**: Environment-based configuration..." → Remove the bold pattern. Write normal bullets.

### Unicode Decoration
Smart quotes, → arrows, and other characters people don't type.
- Use straight quotes, ->, or => instead.

## Composition

### Fractal Summaries
Summarizing at every level: section intro, section, section outro, document conclusion restating everything.
- One conclusion is enough. Trust the reader to remember what they just read.

### The Dead Metaphor
Single metaphor beaten to death across an entire piece.
- Introduce, use, move on. Don't make every paragraph refer back to the same image.

### Historical Analogy Stacking
Rapid-fire listing of companies/revolutions: "Apple didn't build Uber. Facebook didn't build Spotify..."
- One well-chosen example beats five skimmed ones.

### One-Point Dilution
Single argument restated 10 ways across thousands of words.
- Say it once well. Add new supporting evidence, not new phrasings of the same idea.

### Content Duplication
Verbatim or near-verbatim repeated sections in the same piece.
- Delete the duplicate.

### The Signposted Conclusion
"In conclusion...", "To sum up...", "In summary..."
- Just conclude. The reader can tell.

### "Despite Its Challenges..."
Rigid acknowledge-then-dismiss formula: "Despite these challenges, the initiative continues to thrive."
- Engage with the challenges honestly or cut the section.
